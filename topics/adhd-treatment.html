In plain English

Research shows stimulant medications (like methylphenidate, amphetamines, and lisdexamfetamine) are among the most effective treatments for reducing core ADHD symptoms in children, adolescents, and adults, but they commonly cause side effects and raise tolerability and cardiovascular monitoring questions. Non-stimulant and newer agents also show benefit but the strongest, most consistent evidence base across age groups is for stimulants. PMID 30097390 PMID 38523592

Key findings

  • Across many randomized trials, stimulants and some non-stimulant medications reduce ADHD core symptoms compared with placebo; stimulants are ranked among the most efficacious drug classes in network meta-analyses. PMID 30097390 PMID 28700715
  • Systematic reviews covering hundreds of trials conclude medications overall have the strongest evidence for improving ADHD symptoms and related outcomes (including disruptive behaviors), but medications are associated with adverse events. PMID 38523592
  • Short-term randomized trial evidence finds stimulants and lisdexamfetamine have side-effect profiles typical of stimulants (common effects include decreased appetite and insomnia) and small average increases in blood pressure and pulse. Lisdexamfetamine’s safety profile is reported as similar to other stimulants. PMID 24788672
  • Network meta-analyses and large systematic reviews highlight concerns about cardiovascular effects (changes in blood pressure, heart rate) and quantify haemodynamic/ECG outcomes in pediatric trials, indicating the need to consider cardiovascular safety when using ADHD medications. (These analyses included thousands of participants in many short-term trials.) PMID 40203844
  • Newer nonstimulant agents and alternative reuptake inhibitors (for example, centanafadine in adults) have positive randomized trial data showing modest but statistically significant symptom improvements versus placebo; safety/tolerability varied and further data continue to emerge. PMID 35652746
  • Long-term discontinuation is common: narrative reviews note substantial proportions of patients stop ADHD medications within the first year, often because of tolerability or insufficient individual response, motivating development of more treatment options. PMID 38771653 PMID 37587841

How strong is the evidence?

The conclusions come mainly from systematic reviews and network meta-analyses that pooled many randomized controlled trials (for example, analyses including dozens to hundreds of trials and >10,000 participants for short-term efficacy) and from phase 3 randomized trials for newer drugs. Systematic reviews give the highest weight here; single randomized trials (e.g., centanafadine trials) provide supportive but more limited-sample evidence. Some cardiovascular analyses pooled many short-term trials (median follow-up under ~8 weeks in one review), so most high-quality data reflect short-term effects. PMID 30097390 PMID 38523592 PMID 40203844 PMID 35652746

Limitations and caveats

  • These abstracts emphasize short-to-moderate term randomized trials; data on long-term benefits and harms (beyond months to a year) are limited in the summaries provided. PMID 30097390 PMID 40203844
  • Adverse-event rates and cardiovascular effects are reported as averages; individual risk varies and the abstracts do not provide detailed subgroup risk estimates. PMID 24788672 PMID 40203844
  • Although stimulants have the strongest evidence base, comparisons among different stimulants and between stimulants and nonstimulants show few systematic differences in some reviews, meaning choice of agent may depend on tolerability, formulation, and individual response rather than clear superiority. PMID 38523592 PMID 28700715
  • Some newer agents have positive phase 3 data but limited broader post-marketing experience; narrative reviews note ongoing research and the need for more comparative effectiveness and long-term safety data. PMID 35652746 PMID 38771653
