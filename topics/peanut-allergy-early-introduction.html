In plain English

Randomized trials and a systematic review show that introducing peanut-containing foods into an infant’s diet beginning in the first year — often targeted around 4–11 months of age — can substantially reduce the later risk of developing peanut allergy, especially in infants at increased risk (for example, with severe eczema or egg allergy) PMID 25705822 PMID 32396244. Long-term follow-up of one trial found that the protective effect persisted into adolescence for those originally randomized to early peanut consumption PMID 38804779.

Key findings

  • A large randomized trial (LEAP) in high‑risk infants (severe eczema and/or egg allergy) found that consuming peanut from infancy until age 5 years greatly reduced peanut allergy at 60 months compared with avoidance; the effect was seen both in infants with negative and with small positive skin‑prick tests at baseline PMID 25705822.
  • Follow-up of that randomized cohort into adolescence (up to 144 months) showed that participants originally assigned to early peanut consumption had substantially lower prevalence of peanut allergy than those assigned to avoidance, indicating durable tolerance into adolescence PMID 38804779.
  • A randomized trial in a general population of breast‑fed infants that introduced six allergenic foods early (including peanut) showed lower per‑protocol rates of peanut and egg allergy among infants who actually consumed those foods compared with standard introduction, though the intention‑to‑treat result for all six foods combined was not statistically significant; achieving early introduction of all six foods was challenging but appeared safe in the study context PMID 26943128.
  • A systematic review of randomized trials concluded that introducing regular peanut consumption to infants at increased risk beginning roughly 4–11 months “probably” results in a large reduction in peanut allergy; this conclusion was rated as moderate‑certainty evidence and was based on trials in high‑income countries PMID 32396244.
  • Implementation studies indicate that clinician education and clinical decision support can markedly increase adherence to guidelines recommending early peanut introduction, which matters for translating trial results into routine practice PMID 41047140.

How strong is the evidence?

The strongest evidence comes from randomized controlled trials (including a large multicenter trial with 640 infants and other randomized studies) and a systematic review that synthesized randomized trial data; these provide moderate to high‑quality experimental evidence for efficacy in the studied populations PMID 25705822 PMID 26943128 PMID 32396244. Long‑term randomized follow‑up provides additional durability data up to adolescence for one trial cohort PMID 38804779. Narrative reviews summarize and contextualize the findings but are lower on the evidence hierarchy PMID 35807745 PMID 30370459 PMID 42341303.

Limitations and caveats

  • Most trial evidence supporting early peanut introduction comes from infants at increased risk (severe eczema and/or egg allergy) and from high‑income countries; generalizability to all populations and to low‑income settings is less certain PMID 32396244 PMID 25705822.
  • Some trials report differences between intention‑to‑treat and per‑protocol analyses (i.e., benefits were larger among infants who actually consumed the foods), and achieving consistent early introduction in routine practice can be difficult PMID 26943128.
  • While trials show reduced peanut allergy risk, these abstracts do not provide detailed safety guidance (e.g., how to introduce peanut at home safely) or individualized clinical recommendations; they report that interventions in trials were generally safe but do not replace clinical advice PMID 25705822 PMID 26943128.
  • Narrative reviews and policy discussions in the abstracts describe dose suggestions and population‑level effects, but such recommendations in those articles are not substitutes for guideline documents or clinician consultation and may be based partly on extrapolation beyond the randomized trial data PMID 42341303 PMID 35807745.
