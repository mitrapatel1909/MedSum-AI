mRNA vaccines: effectiveness and safety

In plain English

The randomized trials and a meta-analysis in these abstracts show that mRNA vaccines (for COVID-19, influenza, RSV, and combination vaccines) produce strong protection against their target infections and generate robust immune responses. Common side effects are local pain and short-term systemic symptoms (fatigue, headache), and serious adverse events were uncommon and similar to comparators in the trials reported here PMID 33301246 PMID 33378609 PMID 38091530 PMID 41259756 PMID 42090792 PMID 40332892 PMID 39919447 PMID 38282394.

Key findings

  • The BNT162b2 COVID-19 mRNA vaccine showed 95% efficacy against laboratory-confirmed Covid-19 beginning 7 days after the second dose in a large randomized trial (≈43,000 randomized), with mainly short-term mild-to-moderate local and systemic reactions and low, similar rates of serious adverse events in vaccine and placebo groups PMID 33301246.
  • The mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine demonstrated 94.1% efficacy against symptomatic Covid-19 at least 14 days after the second dose in a randomized trial of 30,420 participants; severe Covid-19 cases were much less frequent in vaccine recipients PMID 33378609.
  • A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized and controlled trials (11 studies, 247,186 participants randomized) found that mRNA vaccines provided significant protection versus placebo, with mRNA platforms showing strong pooled protection (reported pooled fold change estimate for mRNA vaccines 0.08, indicating reduced incidence compared with placebo); injection-site discomfort and fatigue were the most common adverse events reported across platforms PMID 38282394.
  • Multiple phase 3 randomized trials of mRNA vaccines for other respiratory pathogens showed clinically meaningful efficacy and acceptable safety: an mRNA RSV vaccine (mRNA-1345) showed high efficacy (around 68–84% against various RSV clinical endpoints) in adults ≥60 and higher solicited local and systemic reactogenicity than placebo PMID 38091530.
  • Phase 3 trials of mRNA influenza vaccines (mRNA-1010 and other modified mRNA influenza vaccines) demonstrated noninferior or superior immunogenicity and relative efficacy versus standard licensed influenza vaccines in adults, with higher rates of solicited adverse reactions but no new safety concerns identified in the reported abstracts PMID 39919447 PMID 42090792 PMID 41259756.
  • A randomized phase 3 trial of a combined influenza + COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (mRNA-1083) in adults ≥50 years showed noninferior immunogenicity to the licensed comparators and acceptable reactogenicity/safety in the enrolled population (8,015 participants) in the abstract summary PMID 40332892.

How strong is the evidence?

The evidence in these abstracts comes mainly from large randomized controlled trials (phase 3 RCTs with tens of thousands of participants for several vaccines) and one systematic review/meta-analysis that pooled randomized or controlled trials (11 studies, ~247,000 participants). Randomized trials provide high-quality evidence about efficacy and typical safety outcomes; the meta-analysis gives pooled estimates across trials. Findings across multiple trials and platforms (mRNA for SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and RSV) are consistent in showing strong immunogenicity/effectiveness and mostly transient reactogenicity.

Limitations and caveats

  • These summaries are based only on the abstracts provided; full trial reports may include more detailed safety signals, subgroup analyses, durability of protection, or rare adverse events that abstracts do not report.
  • Rare adverse events (very low incidence) or long-term safety outcomes may not be detectable in the trial sample sizes or follow-up periods reported in the abstracts. The abstracts report mainly short- to medium-term follow-up.
  • Vaccine efficacy and immune responses reported here refer to the specific formulations, doses, populations, and time periods studied; results may not generalize to different variants, populations not included in the trials, or to real-world observational settings beyond trial conditions.
  • The meta-analysis covers studies up to February 2022 and pooled different vaccine platforms; heterogeneity across trials and changing epidemiology over time can affect pooled estimates.
