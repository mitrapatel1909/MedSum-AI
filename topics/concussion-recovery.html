In plain English

Research on adolescents recovering from sport-related concussion shows most recover within a few weeks, but a substantial minority have symptoms that last months. Early, controlled sub‑symptom aerobic exercise and targeted vestibular/cervicovestibular rehabilitation are supported by randomized trials and reviews as interventions that can speed recovery in adolescents. Pre‑existing anxiety, insomnia, and higher initial symptom burden are associated with slower recovery.

Key findings

  • Across many studies, the pooled mean time to become symptom‑free after sport‑related concussion was about 14 days; return to school typically occurred earlier (mean ~8 days) and return to sport later (mean ~20 days), though studies were highly heterogeneous PMID 37316183.
  • Two randomized controlled trials in adolescents found that early individually prescribed sub‑symptom aerobic exercise (compared with placebo‑like stretching) improved the likelihood of clinical recovery within weeks after concussion PMID 30715132 PMID 34600629.
  • A systematic review concluded that individualized cervicovestibular or vestibular rehabilitation for adolescents with dizziness/vestibular symptoms can decrease time to medical clearance or return to sport versus rest or subtherapeutic interventions PMID 37316188.
  • Pre‑injury or concurrent mental health problems—especially anxiety—and greater initial symptom burden predict more severe symptoms and longer recovery (delayed return to school and sport) in children and adolescents PMID 32788258 PMID 37316183.
  • Among adolescents with slow recovery, insomnia is common (62% above a clinical cutoff in one sample) and is associated with worse post‑concussion symptoms, more anxiety/depression, and more cognitive complaints (but not necessarily worse objective cognitive test performance) PMID 30887895.
  • Parents report that managing cognitive rest, school accommodations, and teamwork among clinicians, schools, and families are important parts of return‑to‑school processes after concussion; qualitative data highlight variability in policies and supports PMID 34806778.

How strong is the evidence?

The evidence includes large systematic reviews/meta‑analyses (278 studies pooled for time‑frame estimates) and multiple randomized controlled trials for the exercise intervention, which gives moderate-to-strong experimental support for early sub‑symptom aerobic exercise in adolescents PMID 37316183 PMID 30715132 PMID 34600629. The systematic review of targeted treatments included 10 RCTs overall and supports vestibular/cervicovestibular rehabilitation for vestibular symptoms, but heterogeneity across studies limited pooled estimates PMID 37316188. Several observational and clinic‑based cohort studies identify predictors (anxiety, insomnia, initial symptom burden), which show consistent associations but are not proof of causation PMID 32788258 PMID 30887895.

Limitations and caveats

  • The pooled recovery time estimates come from many observational studies with high heterogeneity and varying quality; point estimates should be interpreted cautiously PMID 37316183.
  • Randomized trials supporting early aerobic exercise were done in adolescent athletes presenting within 10 days of injury; applicability to non‑athletes or those seen later after injury is uncertain based on these abstracts PMID 30715132 PMID 34600629.
  • Evidence for interventions (vestibular/cervicovestibular rehab) is promising but derived from a limited number of trials with varying designs and outcomes; systematic reviewers could not always meta‑analyse results PMID 37316188.
  • Observational findings linking anxiety, insomnia, and other factors to delayed recovery show associations from clinic samples and cannot by themselves prove these factors cause longer recovery; sample characteristics (e.g., tertiary clinic patients) may limit generalizability PMID 32788258 PMID 30887895
