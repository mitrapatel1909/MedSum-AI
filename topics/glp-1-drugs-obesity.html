In plain English

Clinical trials show that GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs (especially semaglutide, given weekly by injection or daily orally in trials) produce substantial average weight loss in adults with overweight or obesity. Reviews and recent articles describe that newer GLP-1–based and multi-receptor drugs can produce even larger weight losses and have effects beyond weight (for example on glucose and cardiovascular risk), but those broader claims are described in narrative reviews rather than pooled trial evidence in these abstracts PMID 37385278 PMID 35015037 PMID 39160334 PMID 41054801.

Key findings

  • In a 68-week randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled phase 3 trial, oral semaglutide escalated to 50 mg daily produced a mean bodyweight change of −15.1% versus −2.4% with placebo (treatment difference −12.7 percentage points) in adults with overweight or obesity without diabetes PMID 37385278.
  • In a 68‑week randomized phase 3b trial (STEP 8, open‑label comparison of active drugs), once‑weekly subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg led to greater weight loss than once‑daily liraglutide 3.0 mg in adults with overweight or obesity without diabetes (trial compared percent weight change and categorical weight‑loss thresholds) PMID 35015037.
  • Narrative reviews summarize that highly potent GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) have transformed obesity treatment, with reported typical weight losses in the range of roughly 15–25% in trials and accompanying improvements in glycemia and cardiovascular risk markers; these sources also discuss mechanisms (appetite/satiety, central nervous system effects) and the development of new agents (oral forms, multi‑agonists) PMID 39160334 PMID 37445623 PMID 41054801.
  • Preclinical and clinical-safety evidence about GLP‑1 agonist use in pregnancy/lactation is limited; animal studies suggest fetal growth effects and skeletal findings, and human data are scarce, highlighting safety uncertainty in pregnancy and breastfeeding PMID 37881498.
  • Broader benefits of GLP‑1–based medicines beyond weight loss (weight‑independent metabolic and organ effects) are discussed in recent research and reviews, but these are presented as emerging or mechanistic findings rather than pooled clinical trial outcomes in these abstracts PMID 39024455 PMID 39160334.

How strong is the evidence?

The strongest evidence in these abstracts comes from randomized controlled trials: a large phase 3 double‑blind trial of oral semaglutide (n≈667 randomized) and a phase 3b randomized trial comparing injected semaglutide and liraglutide (n=338) PMID 37385278 PMID 35015037. Several narrative reviews and a research article synthesize mechanisms and broader implications but are not systematic pooled analyses; one systematic umbrella review included GLP‑1 receptor agonists among many factors for hepatocellular carcinoma risk but did not focus on obesity outcomes PMID 39834076 PMID 39160334 PMID 37445623 PMID 39024455. Overall, randomized trials provide high‑quality evidence for meaningful weight loss with GLP‑1 agonists; other claims (long‑term outcomes, pregnancy safety, extra‑metabolic benefits) rely more on narrative summaries, preclinical data, or emerging research.

Limitations and caveats

  • These abstracts do not provide long‑term maintenance data beyond the ~68‑week trial periods reported, so durability of weight loss after stopping therapy is not addressed here PMID 37385278 PMID 35015037.
  • Safety details and adverse‑event profiles are only summarized or not fully reported in the provided abstracts; systematic safety synthesis is not available in these excerpts PMID 37385278 PMID 35015037 PMID 37881498.
  • Reviews and narrative articles discuss mechanisms and new drug candidates, but narrative reviews are not the same as systematic reviews or meta‑analyses and may summarize emerging or preclinical findings that need confirmation in trials PMID 39160334 PMID 41054801 PMID 37445623.
  • Pregnancy and lactation data are limited and include animal findings that may not generalize to humans; human data are scarce in the cited systematic review PMID 37881498.
