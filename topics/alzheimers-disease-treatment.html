In plain English

Research shows several different approaches to treating or slowing Alzheimer disease (AD). Exercise and some non‑invasive brain stimulation combined with cognitive training have shown short‑term benefits for cognition, mood, and physical function; monoclonal anti‑amyloid antibodies (example: donanemab) reduced brain amyloid and produced a small slowing of clinical decline in an early randomized trial; dietary patterns like the Mediterranean diet are associated with lower risk of cognitive decline in observational studies and pooled analyses. Many other strategies (drug delivery methods, combination therapies, AI tools, and targets such as tau or neuroinflammation) are described as promising but are not yet proven disease‑modifying treatments in humans.

Key findings

  • Exercise interventions in patients with established AD improved activities of daily living (Barthel index), walking distance (6-minute walk test), neuropsychiatric symptoms, and—particularly for aerobic exercise—showed small improvements on the MMSE cognitive test PMID 34601135.
  • Non‑invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) combined with cognitive training produced small-to-moderate improvements in global cognition in pooled trials of people with AD and mild cognitive impairment; rTMS plus cognitive training showed a consistent benefit in the meta‑analysis PMID 38937842.
  • In a phase 2 randomized controlled trial, the anti‑amyloid antibody donanemab cleared amyloid plaques substantially and produced a small but statistically significant slowing of decline on a composite clinical scale (iADRS) over 76 weeks (difference ~3.2 points) versus placebo; most secondary clinical outcomes showed no substantial differences PMID 33720637.
  • Meta-analysis of observational studies linked higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet with lower hazards of cognitive impairment, dementia, and specifically AD (pooled hazard ratio for AD ~0.70), indicating an association between this dietary pattern and reduced risk PMID 39797935.
  • Reviews and commentaries highlight that current standard drugs (cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine) are symptomatic, that disease‑modifying treatments remain limited, and that future directions include improved drug delivery (e.g., nanocarriers, intranasal routes), combination therapies, AI‑driven diagnostics and personalization, and new targets such as tau and neuroinflammation—all described as promising but not yet definitive therapies PMID 36430432 PMID 40442885 PMID 39293530 PMID 30689575.

How strong is the evidence?

  • The strongest interventional evidence here comes from randomized controlled trials and meta‑analyses of randomized trials: a phase 2 randomized trial supports donanemab's biological effect on amyloid and a small clinical benefit PMID 33720637, and a meta‑analysis of RCTs supports multi‑domain benefits of exercise in people with AD (28 studies, n=1337 pooled; 21 studies meta‑analyzed) PMID 34601135.
  • A meta‑analysis of 15 trials (n=685) supports modest cognitive benefits of NIBS plus cognitive training across AD and MCI PMID 38937842.
  • The Mediterranean diet finding is from a meta‑analysis of observational cohort studies (23 studies pooled), which shows associations but cannot prove causation PMID 39797935.
  • Narrative reviews summarize mechanistic ideas and future directions but do not provide pooled trial evidence; they are useful for context but lower in the evidence hierarchy PMID 36430432 PMID 40442885 PMID 39293530 PMID 30689575.

Limitations and caveats

  • The donanemab trial was phase 2 and showed a small clinical effect despite large amyloid reductions; longer and larger phase 3/real‑world data are needed to confirm meaningful clinical benefit and safety PMID 33720637.
  • The Mediterranean diet meta‑analysis is based on observational studies, so the association could be influenced by confounding (other healthy behaviors) and cannot establish causality PMID 39797935.
  • Exercise and NIBS meta‑analyses include heterogeneous trials (different modalities, durations, and outcome measures), so effects vary by intervention type and study quality PMID 34601135 PMID 38937842.
  • Reviews describing new technologies, combination therapies, and AI summarize promising avenues but do not replace randomized trial evidence; many proposed approaches remain preclinical or early‑stage PMID 36430432 PMID 39293530 PMID 30689575 PMID 40442885.
