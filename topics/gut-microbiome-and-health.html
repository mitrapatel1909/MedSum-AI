In plain English

Research shows the gut microbiome (the community of microbes living in the gut) is strongly influenced by antibiotics, diet, and maternal factors, and that those changes are linked to metabolic, immune, and even mental-health outcomes. Most of the evidence comes from observational studies, reviews, and a few randomized trials showing diet and antibiotic exposures change microbial diversity and composition; causal mechanisms are still being worked out.

Key findings

  • Antibiotic exposure in children is associated with reduced gut microbiome diversity and richness, changes in specific taxa (reduced Bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus; increased Proteobacteria like E. coli), and macrolide antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin) produce larger and longer reductions in diversity than penicillins PMID 33651651.
  • Maternal exposures during pregnancy (intrapartum antibiotics, higher pre-pregnancy BMI, and excessive gestational weight gain) are associated with reduced alpha diversity in the infant gut microbiome, based on pooled observational studies PMID 33978558.
  • Diet and meal patterns alter gut microbiome composition and metabolites: a randomized trial in adults with overweight/obesity found a healthy low-carbohydrate diet and time-restricted eating produced weight loss and distinct changes in the fecal metabolome and microbial taxa (e.g., decreased fecal branched-chain amino acids with low-carb; TRE tended to increase short-chain fatty acid–producing probiotic species) beyond calorie restriction PMID 39454570.
  • Narrative and review-level evidence links overall diet composition (types of foods and macronutrient patterns) to rapid shifts in gut microbiota and to downstream effects on intestinal physiology, immunity, and metabolic disease risk; multiple reviews emphasize diet as a modulatable factor shaping the microbiome [PMID 36076980; PMID 28388917; PMID 32887946].
  • The gut microbiome is implicated in gut–brain communication and mental health through metabolites, hormones, and neural pathways; probiotic strains like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are discussed in reviews as having some impact on mental health symptoms, though sustained colonization is challenging PMID 39273008.

How strong is the evidence?

Most evidence here comes from systematic reviews/meta-analyses of observational studies and from narrative reviews, with fewer randomized controlled trials. The antibiotics–child microbiome conclusion includes meta-analysis and several RCTs and cohort studies PMID 33651651, and the maternal–infant findings come from a meta-analysis of observational studies PMID 33978558. Dietary effects are supported by mechanistic discussion and by one 96-participant randomized feeding trial reporting microbiome and metabolome changes PMID 39454570. Review articles synthesize animal, human observational, and some mechanistic work but do not establish definitive causal pathways for many health outcomes [PMID 32887946; PMID 36076980; PMID 28388917; PMID 39273008].

Limitations and caveats

  • These abstracts do not prove that microbiome changes cause the health outcomes reported; many included studies are observational and show associations rather than causation [PMID 33978558; PMID 32887946].
  • The extent, duration, and clinical importance of microbiome changes (e.g., after antibiotics or dietary change) vary by antibiotic class, timing, and individual factors; details beyond what is summarized are not provided in the abstracts [PMID 33651651; PMID 39454570].
  • Reviews point to many plausible mechanisms (metabolites, immune modulation, gut–brain signaling), but mechanistic and long-term interventional data remain limited in humans [PMID 32887946; PMID 39273008].
  • The randomized trial cited studied adults with overweight/obesity over 12 weeks (plus follow-up) and may not generalize to other populations, ages, or longer-term outcomes; many review conclusions synthesize heterogeneous studies with variable methods [PMID 39454570; PMID 33978558].
