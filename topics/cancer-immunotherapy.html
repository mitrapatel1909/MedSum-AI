In plain English

Recent research shows immunotherapy is an important and effective class of cancer treatments, with clear benefits in some cancers and ongoing work to extend and improve its use. Evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses supports adding immunotherapy to other treatments (for example, chemotherapy before surgery in lung cancer) and indicates that responses vary by cancer type, patient factors, and tumor biology. Other work reviews mechanisms (e.g., lipid metabolism, different T cell types) that may help explain why some tumors respond and how to make immunotherapy work better.

Key findings

  • Neoadjuvant (pre-surgery) chemoimmunotherapy for non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) improved overall survival, event-free survival, and pathological response rates compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone in pooled randomized trials PMID 38512301.
  • Immunotherapy outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) appear to depend on etiology: pooled trial data suggested immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy was more effective in patients with viral hepatitis–related HCC than in nonviral HCC PMID 34126063.
  • Meta-analysis of randomized trials found that immune checkpoint inhibitors produce meaningful overall survival benefits in advanced cancers, but efficacy differed between men and women in pooled data (sex-related heterogeneity was examined) PMID 29778737.
  • Reviews highlight that tumor-intrinsic factors such as lipid metabolic reprogramming in the tumor microenvironment can shape immune responses and affect sensitivity to immunotherapy; targeting lipid metabolism is proposed as a strategy to sensitize tumors to immune therapies (mechanistic and preclinical rationale summarized) PMID 37064872.
  • Alternative immune-cell platforms and modalities—such as γδ T cells and integrative/systems approaches to designing therapies—are under active investigation and show promise in early clinical and translational work, though challenges remain [PMID 39361750; PMID 31311655].
  • For some cancers with strongly immunosuppressive microenvironments (example discussed: pancreatic cancer), current immunotherapies have limited efficacy and combination or novel strategies are being explored to overcome resistance PMID 29860986.

How strong is the evidence?

  • The strongest evidence in these abstracts comes from meta-analyses and pooled randomized clinical trials, for example the NSCLC neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy meta-analysis that pooled data from 8 randomized trials and larger observational series totaling thousands of patients PMID 38512301, and the HCC meta-analysis of phase III randomized trials including over 22,000 patients screened with focused meta-analysis on 3,739 patients for etiology effects PMID 34126063.
  • Other conclusions come from systematic reviews/meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (sex differences in checkpoint inhibitor efficacy) or from narrative reviews that synthesize laboratory, translational, and early clinical data (lipid metabolism, γδ T cells, integrative approaches) [PMID 29778737; PMID 37064872; PMID 39361750; PMID 31311655]. Narrative reviews summarize mechanisms and proposals but do not provide pooled trial evidence.

Limitations and caveats

  • These abstracts do not provide patient-level details (e.g., specific tumor histologies, exact PD-L1 cutoffs beyond mentioning controversy for PD-L1 <1%), so applicability to an individual patient or subgroup cannot be determined from these summaries alone PMID 38512301.
  • Mechanistic and review articles (lipid metabolism, γδ T cells, integrative approaches) synthesize preclinical and early clinical data; they suggest hypotheses and strategies but do not prove clinical benefit in large randomized trials [PMID 37064872; PMID 39361750; PMID 31311655].
  • Sex differences and etiology effects reported in meta-analyses indicate heterogeneity in benefit, but these findings reflect pooled data across diverse trials and cancers; they do not by themselves establish causal mechanisms or prescribe treatment choices [PMID 29778737; PMID 34126063].
  • Narrative reviews noting limited efficacy in certain cancers (e.g., pancreatic cancer) summarize current challenges and possible combination approaches, but do not report successful randomized outcomes for those strategies in the abstracts provided PMID 29860986.
